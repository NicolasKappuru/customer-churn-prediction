\section{Executive Summary}
Customer Churn is a problem about customer discontinue a company's services or products. Machine learning techniques can be applied with purpose of predict if a customer 
has probability of be churn, this information can help the company to take decisions for not lost clients. This work search explore different machine learning techniques
to compare and get the best approach to solve this problem.

\subsection{Dataset Source and Acquisition}

The dataset is the  \textbf{WSDM -- KKBox Music Service Churn Prediction Challenge}, 
publicly available through the Kaggle competition platform at 
\\ \url{https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/kkbox-churn-prediction-challenge}. 
\\ The dataset was released by KKBox Co., Ltd.\ under the competition's terms of use, which permit academic and non-commercial research purposes. No paid subscription is required; a free Kaggle account is sufficient to access all files.
To reproduce this experiment, the following two steps are required:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Accept the competition terms:} Create a free Kaggle account, navigate to the competition page, and accept the data usage rules. This step is mandatory before any file can be downloaded.

    \item \textbf{Download and place the raw files:} Download all nine \texttt{.csv.7z} archives provided by the competition and place them, \textbf{without extracting}, in the directory \texttt{workshops/data/}. The preprocessing pipeline extracts and converts them automatically during execution.
\end{enumerate}

No further manual setup is required. The data pipeline reads the compressed archives directly from \texttt{workshops/data/} and writes the intermediate \texttt{.parquet} files to \texttt{workshops/data/parquet/}. The final processed cohorts are produced in \texttt{workshops/w1/export-tabular-v5/}.

\subsection{Target Distribution}

In the February development cohort 63{,}471 of 992{,}931 customers churn (6.39\%), a majority-to-minority ratio of 14.64:1 (Figure~\ref{fig:w1_target}). In the March test cohort the rate is higher, 8.99\% (87{,}330 of 970{,}960 customers). The target is therefore strongly imbalanced, and the imbalance changes between months.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{figures/target_distribution.png}
\caption{Target distribution in the February development cohort (\texttt{is\_churn}).}
\label{fig:w1_target}
\end{figure}

Two consequences shape the rest of the work. First, accuracy is uninformative: always predicting ``stays'' is 93.6\% accurate in February and 91.0\% accurate in March. The models are instead compared with log-loss, average precision (AP), and the precision, recall, F1 and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) of the churn class. Second, the relationship plots show the churn rate within each group against the overall rate (dashed line) rather than raw counts.

Many activity and payment aggregates are heavily right-skewed, so the median and the 99th percentile describe them better than the mean and maximum. The exploration of the raw tables (notebooks~02--05) adds the following observations:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Members.} There are 21 cities and city~1 dominates. Age (\texttt{bd}) contains negative values and values above 100, and its median is 0 because a missing age was stored as 0. Gender is almost entirely empty, so it is not used as a feature. Registration has 18 channels, with codes 3, 4, 7 and 9 the most frequent.
    \item \textbf{Transactions.} Almost every plan lasts 30 days, although some plans reach about 450 days. The list price and the amount paid are almost identical; where they differ, the difference concentrates at 30, 149 and 180 and sometimes the customer pays more than the list price. Auto-renew is active in most transactions and cancellations are infrequent.
    \item \textbf{Listening logs.} The play counters have very long tails, and values above 1{,}200 songs in a day are implausible. Listening is bimodal: songs are either skipped before 25\% or played to the end. The number of records per user is also bimodal (very few or many).
    \item \textbf{Dates.} Registrations grow strongly from year to year, and transactions and expiry dates follow the 30-day renewal cycle. Time therefore carries information and is encoded as features such as tenure, recency and days to expiry.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Data Quality}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{No member record.} 115{,}770 customers (11.7\%) have no member record, so \texttt{city}, \texttt{tenure\_days} and the registration fields are null for them. The flag \texttt{has\_member\_record} marks them.
    \item \textbf{No transactions.} 2{,}095 customers (0.2\%) have no transaction history and their transaction statistics are null. They are a very high-risk group: 31.6\% of them churn, almost five times the overall rate.
    \item \textbf{Invalid age.} The flag \texttt{bd\_missing} is 1 for 603{,}791 customers (60.8\%), whose raw age is invalid or absent. It is informative in itself: these customers churn \emph{less} (4.8\% versus 8.8\%), so the flag is kept next to the imputed age.
    \item \textbf{Duplicates and invalid counters.} Exact duplicate transactions are removed and negative play counts are set to zero (see the preprocessing pipeline).
\end{itemize}

Missing values are kept in the analysis and dropped pairwise in each measure, so that no value is invented before modelling.

\subsection{Feature Relationships}\label{sec:w1_eda_relationships}

\paragraph{Numeric features.} Figure~\ref{fig:w1_numcorr} shows the Pearson and Spearman correlation of every numeric feature with churn. The strongest monotonic signals come from the subscription state: \texttt{last\_is\_auto\_renew} ($\rho=-0.378$), \texttt{is\_auto\_renew\_change\_flag} ($-0.348$) and \texttt{autorenew\_not\_cancel} ($-0.236$) are negatively associated with churn, while \texttt{last\_is\_cancel} ($+0.270$) is positively associated. Longer plans and higher prices go with more churn (\texttt{last\_plan\_days} $+0.210$, \texttt{average\_amount\_charged} $+0.172$, \texttt{average\_amount\_paid} $+0.148$), whereas a longer payment history goes with less churn (\texttt{transaction\_count} $-0.142$, \texttt{more\_than\_30} $-0.139$). The listening features are individually weak: none exceeds $|\rho|=0.05$.

\paragraph{Implications for modelling.} (i) The strong and shifting imbalance requires threshold-independent metrics and an explicit decision threshold. (ii) The signal is concentrated in the subscription state (auto-renew, plan, payment method and payment history); listening behaviour adds little on its own. (iii) Non-linear and threshold-like patterns, together with categorical levels with very different rates, favour tree-based models. (iv) Redundant feature blocks call for regularisation in the linear model.

\section{Preprocessing Pipeline}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────

The preprocessing pipeline implemented in this work is partially based on the public analysis published by Asad Rizvi~\cite{rizvi2018kkbox}, which includes an exploratory data analysis and a feature engineering approach for the KKBox Churn Prediction Challenge. From his work we adopted the general structure for joining the relational tables and several feature engineering ideas at the transaction and listening level. However, his approach targeted a different modelling objective and proposed a larger feature set with a different temporal framing. We discarded a significant portion of his proposed features, simplified the listening aggregation scheme, and redesigned the cohort structure around a month-over-month temporal split that better reflects a real production scoring scenario.

The full pipeline is implemented in \texttt{workshops/w1/w1\_data\_preprocessing.ipynb}. The model training and evaluation that consumes the pipeline output is in \texttt{workshops/w1/w1\_tabular\_models.ipynb}.

\subsection{Raw Extraction and Parquet Conversion}

The nine \texttt{.csv.7z} archives in \texttt{workshops/data/} are extracted and converted to Parquet format by the \texttt{convert\_raw\_to\_parquet()} function (\texttt{workshops/w1/src/data\_processing/utils.py}), called in Section~2 of the preprocessing notebook. Each archive is decompressed one at a time using the \texttt{7z} binary, and only the columns defined in the project schema are retained and cast to compact integer or float types. The resulting files are written with \texttt{zstd} compression to \texttt{workshops/data/parquet/}. The conversion is idempotent: existing Parquet files are skipped, so the step can be safely resumed after an interruption.

Parquet was chosen over CSV because it is a columnar binary format that stores type information natively. Reading a single column from a large Parquet file does not require parsing the entire file, which reduces both I/O and memory usage significantly compared to CSV for the multi-million-row tables in this dataset.

\subsection{Cohort Definition and User Index}

The dataset is structured around two labelled cohorts defined in Section~3 of the preprocessing notebook:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{February development cohort} (\texttt{train.csv}): 992{,}931 users with features cut at 2017-01-31 and churn labels for February 2017. Churn rate: 6.4\%.
    \item \textbf{March test cohort} (\texttt{train\_v2.csv}): 970{,}960 users with features cut at 2017-02-28 and churn labels for March 2017. Churn rate: 9.0\%.
\end{itemize}

A shared integer index (\texttt{uid}) is assigned over the union of both cohorts (1{,}082{,}190 unique users), replacing the raw \texttt{msno} string for all subsequent joins. A user who appears in both months receives the same \texttt{uid} but a different cutoff, feature snapshot, and label, which mirrors monthly scoring in production.

\subsection{Member Features}

Static demographic features are extracted from \texttt{members\_v3.parquet} in Section~4 of the preprocessing notebook. Invalid ages (outside the range [1, 100]) and negative registration channel values are imputed with the mean age and mode channel computed exclusively on the February cohort. Three derived features are added: \texttt{tenure\_days} (days elapsed from the registration date to the feature cutoff), \texttt{registration\_year}, and \texttt{registration\_month}. A binary flag \texttt{bd\_missing} marks rows where the original age was invalid or absent. This domain contributes \textbf{7 features} per user.

\subsection{Transaction Features}

Transaction records from both \texttt{transactions.parquet} and \texttt{transactions\_v2.parquet} are concatenated and deduplicated in Sections~5--6 of the preprocessing notebook. Exact duplicates—rows identical across all eight transaction fields for the same user—are removed, keeping only the first occurrence. Per-record derived columns are then computed: plan duration, plan coverage ratio, discount (list price minus amount paid), and behavioural flags. These records are subsequently aggregated into \textbf{one row per user}, yielding approximately 29 features covering counts, sums, averages, last-record attributes, and timing features relative to the cutoff (e.g., \texttt{days\_to\_expire}, \texttt{days\_since\_last\_transaction}). All transaction history up to the cohort cutoff is used; no fixed time window is applied.

\subsection{Listening Log Features}

Daily listening logs from both \texttt{user\_logs.parquet} and \texttt{user\_logs\_v2.parquet} are processed in Sections~7--8 of the preprocessing notebook. Records are restricted to the three calendar months ending at the feature cutoff and filtered to target users. Negative play counts are set to zero and non-finite \texttt{total\_secs} values are replaced by zero. The resulting user-day table is grouped to monthly sums and active-day counts. A daily p99.9 clipping limit is applied to the per-day mean of each listening counter—these limits are fitted exclusively on the February cohort and reused for March to prevent leakage.

The last three active months for each user are then pivoted into a wide row (\texttt{last\_*}, \texttt{last\_second\_*}, \texttt{last\_third\_*}). Several redundant per-channel columns are dropped, and four month-over-month trend features are added: \texttt{trend\_songs}, \texttt{trend\_secs}, \texttt{trend\_ratio100}, and \texttt{trend\_ratio25}. Recency features (\texttt{days\_since\_last\_log}, \texttt{has\_listening\_logs}) are computed relative to the cutoff. This domain contributes approximately \textbf{23 features} per user.

\subsection{Final Assembly and Saved Outputs}

The three feature domains are assembled in Sections~9--10 of the preprocessing notebook by left-joining member, transaction, and listening features onto the user list. Users absent from a given domain receive zero for all count and sum columns; presence flags (\texttt{has\_member\_record}, \texttt{has\_transactions}, \texttt{has\_listening\_logs}) mark whether a record existed. Table~\ref{tab:features59} lists the resulting \textbf{59 features} per user together with their source table.

\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}

\section{Data Partitioning}

The split protocol is \texttt{cross\_month\_feb\_to\_mar}: the model is developed on one month and evaluated on the next, which mimics deployment, where a model trained on past customers must score a new month. Two cohorts are exported by the preprocessing pipeline, with the same 59 features but different observation cutoffs (Table~\ref{tab:w1_split}).

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Deterministic assignment.} Each customer is assigned to a part from a SHA-1 hash of \texttt{msno} with a fixed salt, so the assignment does not depend on row order or on a random state, and it is reproducible across machines. It is stratified by the label, so the three February parts keep the same churn rate (6.39\%).
    \item \textbf{Roles.} \emph{fit} trains the parameters of each model. \emph{stop} is used only by XGBoost to stop adding trees when the validation loss stops improving. \emph{tune} (the validation set) is where Optuna evaluates hyperparameters, where the decision threshold is chosen and where the winning model is selected. Separating \emph{stop} from \emph{tune} prevents the early-stopping criterion from leaking into the hyperparameter comparison.
    \item \textbf{Test set.} March is never used to fit, tune or choose a threshold. It is used once, for the final metrics.
    \item \textbf{No leakage from preprocessing.} Imputation medians, scaling statistics and category encodings are learned on the fit/development data only and stored (\texttt{preprocessing/development.json}); the test cohort is transformed with those fixed values.
    \item \textbf{Final model.} After the hyperparameters and the threshold are chosen, each model is refitted on all of February (fit, stop and tune together, 992{,}931 customers) before scoring March.
\end{itemize}

Because the test set is a different month, it also measures robustness to a change of the churn rate (6.39\% to 8.99\%); this shift matters for the interpretation of the results.

\section{Models and Hyperparameter Tuning}

\subsection{Baseline Models}

Three models with increasing capacity are compared on the same 59 features and the same split.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{M1 -- Logistic regression.} Numeric features are median-imputed and standardised; the nominal codes are one-hot encoded (unseen categories ignored). It is fitted with the \texttt{newton-cholesky} solver with at most 200 iterations. It is an interpretable linear reference whose only tuned parameter is the inverse regularisation strength $C$.
    \item \textbf{M2 -- Random Forest.} An ensemble of decision trees trained on bootstrap samples with random feature subsets \cite{breiman2001random}. It handles non-linear effects and interactions without scaling and is robust to the redundancy between features.
    \item \textbf{M3 -- XGBoost.} Gradient-boosted trees \cite{chen2016xgboost} fitted on GPU with early stopping on the \emph{stop} part (up to 3{,}000 rounds, patience 100). To favour recall, the positive class is up-weighted with \texttt{scale\_pos\_weight} equal to twice the natural ratio of negatives to positives (about 29.3 on the fit data).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Tuning with Optuna}

Hyperparameters are optimised with Optuna \cite{akiba2019optuna} using the TPE sampler (seed 110) and a median pruner. The objective is the log-loss on the \emph{tune} part, which is a proper scoring rule and does not depend on a threshold. Studies are stored in SQLite so that interrupted runs resume. The first trial of every study is a fixed, sensible starting configuration, so the search can only improve on it. Table~\ref{tab:w1_space} gives the search spaces and the best values found.

\paragraph{Probability scale of M3.} The tune log-loss of M3 (0.494) is not comparable with M1 and M2 (0.155 and 0.145): up-weighting the churners makes XGBoost's scores systematically larger than the true probabilities, which inflates the log-loss. Table~\ref{tab:w1_select} therefore selects the winner on the tune log-loss among the models whose scores are probabilities, and the ranking metrics (ROC AUC, AP) are used to compare all three.

The default 0.5 is inappropriate here, because with 6\% positives most calibrated probabilities fall below 0.5. \subsection{Model Comparison}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{M2 (Random Forest) is the best overall.} It has the highest ROC AUC (0.863), AP (0.593), precision, F1 and MCC, and the lowest log-loss and Brier score of the three. It also gives the fewest false alarms.
    \item \textbf{M3 (XGBoost) is very close in ranking.} Its AP (0.588) and AUC (0.852) are within one point of M2, and it has the highest recall (0.542). Its probabilities are not calibrated because of the class weight, so its log-loss and Brier score are much worse and cannot be compared with the other two.
    \item \textbf{M1 (logistic regression) is the weakest} (AP 0.555, F1 0.557) but not by a large margin: a linear model on the engineered features is already competitive, which indicates that most of the signal is in a few strong features (auto-renew, plan, payment method).
    \item \textbf{The differences among models are small compared with the gap to the trivial baselines.} The best AP is 6.6 times the no-skill AP (0.0899), whereas the gap between M2 and M1 is 0.04. With only 3--8 tuning trials per model, ranking the three should be taken as indicative, not definitive.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Why the F1 Score Is Low}

The best F1 for the churn class is 0.58, which looks low next to an accuracy above 92\%. This is expected given the problem and does not by itself indicate a bug:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Accuracy is not informative.} Predicting ``stays'' for everybody gives 91.0\% accuracy in March. The accuracies of 92.3--93.1\% obtained by the models are only about two points above this trivial classifier, while F1 focuses on the minority class.
    \item \textbf{Class imbalance makes precision hard.} With 9\% positives, a model that is right on 54\% of the churners and wrong on 3\% of the stayers (M2: recall 0.539, false-positive rate 0.030) has a precision of only 0.64, because there are ten times more stayers than churners to make mistakes on. The precision--recall trade-off is also stiff: the AP of 0.59 means that precision drops quickly as recall grows (Figure~\ref{fig:w1_roc_pr}).
    \item \textbf{ROC AUC looks good while F1 does not.} An AUC of 0.86 means that a random churner is ranked above a random non-churner 86\% of the time, but the ROC curve is insensitive to the number of negatives; AP and F1 are not. The metrics measure different things and are consistent with each other.
    \item \textbf{The threshold was tuned on a month with a different prevalence.} The F1-optimal thresholds were selected on February (6.39\% churn) and applied to March (8.99\%). All three models therefore predict fewer churners than there actually are (7.6--8.3\% against 9.0\%), and the recall stays around 0.54. A threshold tuned on March itself would give a higher recall, so part of the loss is due to the prior shift between months (the same shift raises the log-loss from 0.145 on \emph{tune} to 0.203 on the test set for M2).
    \item \textbf{Limited information.} The features are aggregates of subscription, payment and listening behaviour up to the cutoff. Many customers leave for reasons not recorded in these tables (price, competition, life events), and the listening features carry little individual signal (Section~\ref{sec:w1_eda_relationships}); this puts a ceiling on the achievable F1 regardless of the model.
    \item \textbf{Light tuning.} The small number of Optuna trials leaves some room for improvement, although the extra experiment below suggests that it is limited.
\end{enumerate}

Table~\ref{tab:w1_thr05} shows the effect of the threshold directly. At the default 0.5, M1 and M2 detect only 24--27\% of the churners (precision 0.78--0.83), while M3, whose scores are inflated, flags 21\% of all customers; none of them is useful at 0.5, which justifies tuning the threshold.

\subsection{Additional Experiment: XGBoost v2}

To check whether more tuning would change the picture, a second XGBoost study (M3v2) was run: 80 Optuna trials maximising the average precision on \emph{tune}, a wider search space (growth policy, leaves, column sampling per node, maximum delta step and \texttt{scale\_pos\_weight} in $[1,30]$), and isotonic calibration fitted on \emph{tune}. Its best configuration uses \texttt{scale\_pos\_weight} $=1.90$, i.e.\ almost no re-weighting. Table~\ref{tab:w1_v2} shows that M3v2 reaches the best ROC AUC (0.864) and a log-loss (0.204) now comparable with M2, but its AP (0.590) and its F1 at the F1-optimal threshold (0.576) are not better than M2's (0.593 and 0.584). A recall-oriented threshold ($\geq$0.70 on \emph{tune}) gives a recall of 0.645 at a precision of 0.482 (F1 0.552).

\subsection{Conclusions of Workshop 1}

\begin{itemize}
    \item The churn target is imbalanced (6.4\% in February, 9.0\% in March) and the signal is concentrated in subscription features, mainly auto-renew status, plan length, payment method and payment history.
    \item The partition (fit/stop/tune from February, March as test) keeps the test month untouched and measures robustness to a change of churn rate.
    \item The Random Forest (M2) is selected: ROC AUC 0.863, AP 0.593, F1 0.584 and MCC 0.549 on March. XGBoost is a close second and logistic regression is a competitive linear baseline.
    \item An F1 around 0.58 is a consequence of class imbalance, the precision--recall trade-off, the shift in churn rate between months and the limited information in the features; it is not evidence of a faulty model. The metrics must be read together (AP, MCC, recall and precision), never accuracy alone.
    \item Next steps: a larger Optuna budget, a threshold chosen with the expected prevalence of the target month, and sequence models that use the temporal structure of the logs (Workshop~2).
\end{itemize}
